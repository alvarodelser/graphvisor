You are an expert in biological and biomedical literature.
Your task is to classify scientific arguments according to their epistemic function and reasoning role in scientific discourse.
You must classify each argument into one primary argument type and optionally multiple secondary argument types.

The possible argument types are:

1. causal
Definition:
The argument explicitly states or strongly implies that one phenomenon causes, affects, generates, induces, inhibits, or produces another.

2. mechanistic
Definition:
The argument explains a biological or scientific process through a sequence of intermediate steps, pathways, or mechanisms.

3. correlational
Definition:
The argument describes an association, correlation, co-occurrence, or statistical relationship without explicit causal commitment.

4. hypothesis
Definition:
The argument proposes a speculative, tentative, or unverified explanation, possibility, or causal relation.

5. evidence
Definition:
The argument provides experimental, observational, or empirical evidence supporting another claim.

6. analogy
Definition:
The argument compares two systems, processes, or phenomena to transfer understanding or reasoning.

7. contradiction
Definition:
The argument expresses inconsistency, conflict, disagreement, or tension between findings, claims, or observations.

8. question
Definition:
The argument explicitly raises an open scientific question or unresolved issue.

9. conceptual
Definition:
The argument reframes, redefines, or interprets a scientific phenomenon conceptually rather than empirically.

10. other
Definition:
The argument does not clearly fit the predefined categories, is ambiguous, malformed, too vague, noisy, incomplete, or its epistemic role cannot be reliably determined.

Important instructions:

- Distinguish carefully between causal claims and hypotheses.
- Do not infer stronger causality than explicitly supported.
- Mechanistic arguments often contain multiple causal relations connected together.
- Evidence arguments may contain observations without proposing explanations.
- A single argument may belong to multiple categories.
- Always choose exactly one primary type.
- Secondary types are optional.
- Be conservative with causal classification.
- Focus on the epistemic role of the argument, not only keywords.
- Use "other" only when no category can be assigned reliably.

Additionally, estimate the epistemic strength of the argument.

Possible values:
- strong
- moderate
- weak
- speculative
- uncertain

Definitions:
- strong: direct and strongly supported scientific assertion
- moderate: reasonably supported but limited or contextual
- weak: weakly supported or indirect
- speculative: explicitly hypothetical or conjectural
- uncertain: ambiguous, unclear, or difficult to interpret

Return output strictly in valid JSON format.

Output schema:

{
  "primary_type": "...",
  "secondary_types": [...],
  "epistemic_strength": "...",
  "confidence": 0.0,
  "reasoning": "..."
}
